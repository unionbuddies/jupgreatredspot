The Great Red Spot

The Great Red Spot is a colossal anticyclonic storm — a high-pressure system whirling counter-clockwise in Jupiter's southern hemisphere. It is the largest and longest-lived storm known anywhere in the Solar System, and astronomers have tracked it for well over a century.

How it formed

Jupiter has no solid surface — it's a giant ball of gas that spins astonishingly fast, completing a full day in under 10 hours. That rapid rotation, combined with heat rising from the planet's interior, tears its atmosphere into powerful east–west jet streams, which we see as Jupiter's colorful bands.

The Great Red Spot sits wedged between two of these opposing jet streams — one racing eastward to its north, one westward to its south. Like a marble caught between two conveyor belts, the storm is spun up and hemmed in from both sides, which keeps it tightly organized and continuously fed with energy.

Why it never dies

  • On Earth, hurricanes fade when they hit land or cool water. Jupiter has neither — so nothing breaks the storm apart.
  • It survives by absorbing smaller storms that drift into it, swallowing their energy.
  • Heat welling up from Jupiter's core continuously powers its circulation.

What Juno revealed

NASA's Juno spacecraft, orbiting Jupiter since 2016, peered beneath the cloud tops with microwave and gravity instruments. It found the storm isn't just a surface feature — its roots plunge at least 300–500 km deep into the atmosphere, far deeper than Earth's oceans, though shallower than the storm is wide.

The mystery of its color

No one is completely certain why it's red. The leading idea is that chemicals — possibly sulfur, phosphorus, or organic compounds — are dredged up from deep below and then chemically altered by the Sun's ultraviolet light, staining the high cloud tops a rusty red.

Will it disappear? The storm has been shrinking for over a century, and whether it will eventually fade away — or simply stabilize at a smaller size — is one of planetary science's genuinely open questions.
